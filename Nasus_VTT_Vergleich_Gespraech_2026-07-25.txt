Why Foundry fits Nasus

Nasus is not merely a character sheet plus standard attack rolls. The existing
material indicates:

- a large, structured catalogue of properties, skills, talents, weapons, armour
  and magic;
- calculated and interdependent values;
- unusual die sizes;
- detailed Trefferzonen;
- zonal armour, damage and secondary effects;
- conditional modifiers and rerolls;
- character-development and availability formulas.

Foundry lets a custom system define its own Actors, Items, data models, documents
and rule logic. Its official development model uses JavaScript, HTML, CSS and
JSON, which maps cleanly onto the Nasus spreadsheet/data-pipeline work already
underway. Systems can also ship compendiums for weapons, spells, talents and
other structured records.

Its roller supports arbitrary face counts and complex expressions, plus rerolls,
success counting, keep/drop operations and dynamic variables. That should cover
most Nasus rolls without fighting the platform.

Fantasy Grounds rulesets, by comparison, are built from XML interfaces and Lua
scripts, usually layered over CoreRPG. That provides valuable inherited
machinery, but the learning curve and implementation friction are higher for a
system whose source data and tooling already resemble a web application.

Where Fantasy Grounds is stronger

Fantasy Grounds becomes the better choice if the main objective is:

- extremely deep combat automation from the beginning;
- drag-and-drop targeting, effects and damage workflows patterned after
  established commercial rulesets;
- simple hosted connectivity without configuring a server;
- selling Nasus content directly through an integrated VTT marketplace.

Fantasy Grounds now makes its base VTT free for GMs and players, and FG Forge
supports both free and paid community publishing with automatic updates. Its
built-in combat and campaign tooling is a genuine strength.

The catch is that those automation benefits are strongest in existing supported
rulesets. A new Nasus ruleset would still need custom Lua/XML code to teach
Fantasy Grounds about Trefferzonen, Nasus damage resolution, armour interactions
and its special conditions.

Cost

- Foundry: currently US$50 once for the host; players connect free through a
  browser. The licence includes future updates.
- Fantasy Grounds: the core platform is currently free, including for GMs and
  players; optional rules and content modules are sold separately.
- For Nasus, the platform price is insignificant compared with custom-system
  development and maintenance.

Foundry hosting is flexible—local, cloud or partner-hosted—but local internet play
can require port forwarding or an alternative host when the connection uses
CGNAT.

Practical decision

I would use this direction:

1. Foundry as the canonical Nasus implementation.
2. Build a narrow first release containing:
   - character and NPC sheets;
   - properties, skills and derived values;
   - weapons, armour and inventory;
   - the Nasus roll engine;
   - Trefferzonen and basic damage application.
3. Add advanced combat automation, magic and character advancement incrementally.
4. Keep the source rule data platform-neutral so a Fantasy Grounds export remains
   possible later.

My project-specific score is approximately Foundry 9/10 versus Fantasy Grounds
6.5/10. Fantasy Grounds wins on marketplace infrastructure and mature combat
conventions; Foundry wins on nearly everything that determines whether Nasus can
be implemented cleanly and maintained over time.

ASSISTANT

That clarification makes the decision hinge almost entirely on the combat-round
mechanic, not damage automation.

Your damage pipeline is linear and straightforward to implement on either
platform:

Roll damage
→ deduct armour of the Trefferzone
→ apply remaining SP
→ compare SP with Trefferschwelle
→ if SP > TS: roll Selbstbeherrschung
→ look up consequence
→ apply status/effect

This can be a single “Apply damage” workflow with optional GM confirmation before
the resulting status is committed.

The important question is how far the Nasus combat round differs from conventional
initiative:

- If it is essentially “sort combatants, take turns, advance round,” Fantasy
  Grounds gains value because its Combat Tracker already provides a mature
  foundation.
- If it involves simultaneous declarations, variable action timing, multiple
  phases, reactions, changing action costs, interruptions, or resolution at
  different points within a round, Foundry becomes even more clearly preferable.
  Its combat tracker and interface can be replaced or extended with a
  Nasus-specific timeline rather than adapting Nasus to a pre-existing turn
  structure.
- Fantasy Grounds can also implement a custom round, but this means overriding or
  carefully extending CoreRPG’s combat-management assumptions in Lua. That is
  feasible, but likely to become the highest-maintenance part of the ruleset.

My revised assessment:

Area                                     Foundry          Fantasy Grounds
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
Nasus damage workflow                    9/10             9/10
Trefferzonen and armour                  9/10             8/10
Status lookup and application            9/10             9/10
Conventional combat rounds               8/10            10/10
Highly unconventional combat rounds     10/10             6–7/10

So the simple damage procedure does not create a strong Fantasy Grounds
advantage. The deciding artefact should be a prototype of one complete Nasus
combat round—including declarations, ordering, interruptions and transition to
the next round. If that works cleanly, the rest of the combat system is
comparatively routine.

ASSISTANT

That is a nested combat scheduler, not a conventional initiative tracker. It
strongly favors Foundry.

Round flow:

Roll global initiative
→ Declare actions: lowest to highest
→ Highest initiative declares
→ Highest initiative acts immediately
→ Resolve remaining actions: high to low
→ If actors enter Nahkampf, create or update an NK bubble
→ Determine NK initiative
→ NK declarations: lowest to highest
→ Resolve NK actions: highest to lowest
→ Update NK initiative from the outcome
→ Bubble remains scheduled at its highest initiative
→ Continue the global sequence

The important implementation details are:

- Declaration and execution must be separate records. An actor first stores an
  intended action; the action is only evaluated when its execution point arrives.
- Game effects are transactional. Damage, movement, statuses and target
  availability change only after an action has actually resolved.
- A planned action is not simply discarded when invalidated. The system
  determines why it became invalid and presents only the permitted modifications.
- Nahkampf bubbles are first-class combat entities. Each bubble has members, its
  own initiative order, declarations, current execution point and possibly
  changing internal initiative.
- The global scheduler treats a bubble as occupying the highest global initiative
  currently present inside it.
- Bubble membership and initiative may need recalculation after entry, withdrawal,
  incapacitation, displacement or another combat outcome.

A suitable internal model would be roughly:

Combat
├─ global initiative entries
├─ current phase: declaration | resolution
├─ pending actions
└─ NK bubbles
   ├─ members
   ├─ highest global initiative
   ├─ internal initiative order
   ├─ declarations
   └─ current action

Each planned action should retain:

actor
declared target
declared action
declared parameters
declaration timestamp/order
current validity
allowed modifications
resolution result
effects applied

Platform consequence

Foundry is now the clear choice:

- Its combat interface can be replaced with a Nasus-specific declaration and
  resolution tracker.
- Nahkampf bubbles can have expandable sub-trackers.
- The UI can reveal declarations according to Nasus rules.
- Invalidated actions can open a restricted correction dialogue.
- Every completed action can be logged as an event, making mistakes auditable and
  potentially reversible.
- The scheduler can be tested independently of maps and character sheets.

Fantasy Grounds’ standard Combat Tracker assumes a comparatively flat list of
initiative entries. Implementing Nasus would require replacing much of its
sequencing behaviour while still working inside its CoreRPG framework. Its
existing combat automation therefore offers less benefit than the earlier
comparison suggested.

Recommended prototype

The first prototype should omit damage, armour and detailed character sheets. It
should demonstrate only:

1. Five actors roll initiative.
2. Declarations proceed from lowest to highest.
3. Actions resolve from highest downward.
4. Two actors form a Nahkampf bubble.
5. The bubble runs its own declaration/resolution sequence.
6. Its internal initiative changes.
7. A declared target becomes unavailable.
8. The affected actor receives only the legally permitted alternatives.
9. The round finishes without losing or duplicating an action.

If that scheduler is reliable, the damage sequence you described can be added as
a relatively contained action-resolution module. For Nasus, the scheduler is the
real system core; the character sheet and damage automation should be built
around it.

ASSISTANT

You’re right. Calling the scheduler “the real system core” was too strong.

The core is the Nasus character and rules engine. The scheduler controls when
rules are invoked, but it cannot determine valid actions, costs, movement, Parade,
Ausweichen, Trefferzonen or consequences without the character rules.

A better architecture is:

Nasus rules engine
├─ Character values and derived statistics
├─ Action validation and AP costs
├─ Movement rules
├─ Attack and defence rules
├─ Damage, armour and mitigation
├─ Trefferzonen, Trefferschwelle and statuses
└─ Combat scheduler
   ├─ Global initiative
   ├─ Declaration/execution sequence
   └─ Nahkampf bubbles

Revised round model

Each actor begins a round with up to three action points, as determined by the
character rules.

During declaration, the actor must account for all available AP:

AP 1 → declared action
AP 2 → declared action
AP 3 → declared action

Potentially:

Movement → normally costs AP, but may be reduced to 0
Movement limit → one movement per actor per round
Undeclared/unused AP → lost

The system therefore cannot store only one “planned action.” It needs an ordered
action plan:

Actor plan
├─ available AP
├─ Action 1: type, target, cost and parameters
├─ Action 2: type, target, cost and parameters
├─ Action 3: type, target, cost and parameters
├─ movement already declared/performed
└─ remaining or forfeited AP

Cost must be validated by the character engine at declaration time and checked
again at execution time. Zero-cost movement still consumes the once-per-round
movement allowance even though it consumes no AP.

Attack resolution

Damage remains relatively simple, but the complete attack workflow is larger:

Attack is executed
→ determine attack result and Trefferzone
→ offer or resolve permitted defence
→ determine whether the attack still hits
→ roll damage
→ apply further mitigation
→ deduct TZ armour
→ apply remaining SP
→ compare SP with Trefferschwelle
→ if SP > TS, roll Selbstbeherrschung
→ look up consequence
→ apply status

Defensive options depend on the attack context:

Context       Possible mitigation
----------------------------------------------
Nahkampf      Parade through an NK action
Fernkampf     Glück, Ausweichen, Parieren, or no defence
No defence    Continue directly to damage

This makes defence part of action resolution, not merely part of the damage
formula. The system must ask the rules engine:

What defensive options are legal for this defender right now?
What do they cost?
Were they declared or reserved?
Has the required action/resource already been spent?
Does the defence interrupt, oppose or replace the attack result?

Whether Parade, Ausweichen and the other defences must be predeclared, consume an
already-declared action, or can be selected reactively is especially important.
The data model can support all three, but the scheduler behaviour differs.

Platform consequence

This still favors Foundry, but for a more accurate reason:

- The character sheet and rules engine come first because they define AP, legal
  actions, derived values and costs.
- The scheduler then consumes that rules API instead of duplicating rules.
- Damage is a contained resolution pipeline.
- The custom initiative and Nahkampf-bubble interface is an important subsystem,
  not the foundation of the entire implementation.

A sensible build order would be:

1. Character data model and derived values.
2. Action catalogue, eligibility and AP-cost calculation.
3. Attack and defence resolution.
4. Damage, Trefferzonen, armour, TS and status consequences.
5. Declaration and execution scheduler.
6. Nahkampf bubbles and changing internal initiative.
7. Restricted modification of invalidated plans.

That avoids building an impressive scheduler whose action legality later
conflicts with the actual character rules.
